Key findings

  • Charging is ~4.4× cheaper on average — $52.17/mo vs. $231.53/mo for the same 1,125 miles. Every one of the 51 states saves money by charging.
  • The two distributions are completely separated — the EV maximum ($125.87, Hawaii) sits far below the gas minimum ($196.54, Indiana), so no overlap at all.
  • Both are right-skewed with the same outliers — California and Hawaii breach the upper 1.5×IQR fence in both datasets (gas also flags Washington), and mean > median in both.
  • Gas costs vary more in dollars, EVs more in relative terms — SD $23.69 (CV 10%) for gas vs. SD $18.65 (CV 36%) for charging, because electricity rates differ more between states than gas prices do.
  • Savings range from $148/mo (Indiana) to $249/mo (Washington) — cheap-electricity, expensive-gas states like Washington save most; the two costs correlate only moderately by state (r = 0.56).

Hypothesis test — is the difference real?

Independent two-sample t-test (Welch), left-tailed · α = 0.05
  • Hypotheses: H₀: μEV − μgas = 0  vs.  Ha: μEV − μgas < 0 (left-tailed) — matching our claim that electric vehicles are more cost-efficient than gas vehicles.
  • Result: t = −42.48, df ≈ 94.8. The critical value at α = 0.05 is −1.661, and our t is far into the left tail (p < .001), so we reject H₀ — mean monthly charging cost is significantly lower than mean gas cost.
  • 95% confidence bound (one-sided, matching the left-tailed test) for μEV − μgas: at most −$172.34. Zero is nowhere near the bound, agreeing with the test: we’re 95% confident charging costs at least ~$172/mo less. (Two-sided 95% CI: −$187.74 to −$170.97.)
  • Conditions: both samples have n = 51 ≥ 30 (CLT covers the right-skew and the CA/HI outliers); costs are computed independently within each dataset. Caveat: this is a census of all 51 jurisdictions, not a random sample, and the two groups share the same states.
  • Robustness: because both costs are measured on the same 51 states, a paired t-test on the differences is arguably the better fit — left-tailed it gives t(50) = −63.01, p < .001, 95% upper bound −$174.58. Same conclusion, even stronger.

Conclusion, errors & limitations

Answering the research question: which is more cost-efficient?
  • Answer: for fuel alone, the EV wins everywhere — charging a Model 3 at home costs less than fueling an F-150 in all 51 jurisdictions, by ~$179/mo (~$2,152/yr) on average. The data support the hypothesis.
  • Type I error (concluding EVs are cheaper when they aren’t) is very unlikely here — p < .001 is orders of magnitude below α. Type II doesn’t apply since we rejected H₀.
  • Limitations: fuel/energy cost only — purchase price, insurance, maintenance, depreciation, and battery replacement are excluded; home-charging rates only (DC fast charging costs 2–3× more); truck vs. sedan comparison; state averages hide within-state variation; gas prices are July 2026 while electricity rates are April 2026 (the latest EIA has published — EIA reports ~2–3 months behind).
  • Future work: compare same-class vehicles (e.g., F-150 vs. F-150 Lightning), add hybrids and hydrogen, and use total cost of ownership.
